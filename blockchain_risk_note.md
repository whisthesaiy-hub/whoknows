# Blockchain / Crypto Risk-Analysis Appendix

## Stablecoin type and DeFi/DAO governance risk

Before a hypothetical "Paytm Crypto Insights" watchlist feature could responsibly surface any stablecoin to retail users, it would need to correctly classify and disclose the stablecoin's backing mechanism, because the two dominant types carry fundamentally different risk profiles. A fiat-collateralized stablecoin (for example, one backed 1:1 by cash and short-term government securities held with a regulated custodian) has a relatively transparent, auditable peg, and its main risks are custodial (does the issuer actually hold what it claims, and how quickly could holders redeem in a stress event) and regulatory (is the issuer licensed and subject to reserve-attestation requirements). An algorithmic stablecoin, by contrast, maintains its peg through code-driven supply/demand mechanisms and market incentives rather than a redeemable reserve asset, and history (most visibly the 2022 collapse of an algorithmic stablecoin and its companion token) has shown these pegs can fail suddenly and completely under a bank-run-style dynamic, with no underlying reserve to fall back on. A watchlist feature would need to label this distinction prominently, not bury it in fine print, and should probably default to excluding or clearly red-flagging algorithmic designs given their demonstrated tail risk.

DeFi/DAO tokens add a second, separate risk layer on top of any stablecoin exposure: governance concentration. Many DAOs have token-weighted voting that is, in practice, held by a small number of early investors or the founding team, meaning "decentralized governance" can be largely nominal, and a concentrated holder group can push through protocol changes, treasury withdrawals, or parameter changes that harm smaller token holders with limited recourse. A responsible watchlist would need to surface governance-token concentration (e.g., top-10-holder percentage) and any recent contentious governance votes, not just price and market cap.

## Crypto as an asset class: allocation recommendation for Paytm Money

CAPM-style portfolio theory, as used throughout Part A of this project, is built on the idea that an asset earns its place in an optimal portfolio through some combination of expected return justified by systematic risk (beta) and diversification benefit (low or negative correlation with the rest of the portfolio). Cryptocurrency struggles on both fronts as commonly practiced in retail advisory: it has no intrinsic cash flow or dividend to anchor a fundamental expected-return estimate the way CAPM does for equities; empirically its correlation with traditional risk assets has risen over time rather than staying reliably low or negative, eroding its diversification case exactly when diversification is needed most (during broad risk-off events); its return distribution is heavy-tailed and positively skewed, meaning historical average returns are disproportionately driven by a small number of extreme up-moves that are not a reliable basis for forward-looking allocation math; the space also suffers from acute survivorship bias, since the assets and platforms that no longer exist (including ones that failed catastrophically) are excluded from most retrospective return calculations, flattering the apparent risk-return profile of "crypto" as a category; and transaction/custody costs remain meaningfully higher than for listed securities. Given all of this, my recommendation for a retail advisory product like Paytm Money is a justified zero allocation within the core CAPM-optimized advisory portfolio described in Part A — cryptocurrency exposure, if offered at all, should live in a clearly separated, explicitly-labeled "high-risk / speculative" bucket outside the optimized allocation, sized only from money the investor can afford to lose entirely, and never blended into the risk-tiered portfolios this project's advisory agent produces.

## T.A.N.G. framework: social-engineering risk vectors for a UPI/wallet + lending + wealth platform

Applying the T.A.N.G. (Temptation/Authority/Need/Greed) fraud framework to a combined UPI/wallet + lending + wealth platform, the two vectors I consider most relevant are **Authority** and **Need**.

**Authority** is the highest risk because the platform spans payments, credit, and investing under one trusted brand, giving fraudsters a uniquely credible impersonation target: a call or SMS impersonating "Paytm support," a bank, or a regulator asking a user to "verify" their account, approve a UPI collect request, or share an OTP carries outsized weight precisely because the real platform legitimately does contact users about payments, loan approvals, and KYC. The bank-side real-time defense is **transaction-context risk scoring on UPI collect/approval requests** — flagging and adding a friction step (e.g., a explicit warning screen, not just a PIN prompt) when a collect request or approval pattern matches known social-engineering scripts (e.g., a large amount, a brand-new payee, immediately following an inbound call), rather than relying solely on the user to recognize the scam.

**Need** is the second most relevant vector because Paytm Postpaid/BNPL products specifically target credit-need customers, some of whom are new to formal credit (the "thin-file" population from Part 2), and fraudsters can exploit financial urgency with fake "instant loan" or "loan pre-approval" schemes that mimic the real product's UX to harvest KYC documents or upfront "processing fees." The bank-side real-time defense is **velocity and pattern-based device/account monitoring at the lending-application layer** (the same velocity-attack detection logic built in Part 1) — flagging rapid-fire loan applications from a new device, a brand-new account, or a device previously associated with confirmed fraud, before disbursal, rather than only after a chargeback or default occurs.
